# T.R.A.C.K Writing Style Rules

Use these rules for every rewrite of T.R.A.C.K job cards, program notes, career paths, site copy, and supporting pages.

## Voice

- Write like a careful high school student who knows the topic, not like a marketing team or an encyclopedia.
- Keep the tone formal, clear, and human.
- Sound curious and specific, but do not sound casual, jokey, salesy, or inflated.
- Use plain explanations before technical wording when both are needed.
- Let the writing feel researched, but not polished into corporate language.
- Prefer concrete actions over abstract claims.
- Avoid wording that sounds like the site is ranking schools or guaranteeing career outcomes.

## Sentence Style

- Use mostly direct sentences.
- Mix short and medium sentence lengths.
- Do not use questions in rewritten T.R.A.C.K copy.
- Use commas or new sentences instead of em dashes.
- Do not use em dashes anywhere.
- Avoid long chains of clauses.
- Avoid repeating the same sentence shape across multiple cards.
- Avoid filler openings like "In today's industry" unless the date or context actually matters.

## Job Descriptions

- Start with what the person actually does.
- Name the real work, tools, setting, or output as quickly as possible.
- If a title covers more than one route, explain the difference clearly.
- Do not make the job sound larger than it is.
- Do not make a job sound easier or more certain than it is.
- Do not add editorial comments, judgments, opinions, or value claims.
- If a role is usually an outcome of another path, say that in the career path instead of creating a separate card.
- If a role is regulated, licensed, union-based, or portfolio-based, say so plainly.

## Key Responsibilities

- Use action verbs that describe the real task.
- Keep each responsibility specific enough that a student can picture the work.
- Avoid vague items like "support operations" unless the sentence names what support means.
- Do not combine unrelated responsibilities just to make a list shorter.
- Use semicolons only when the field requires one-line list formatting.

## Career Paths

- Keep the numbered format.
- Make the first step about the actual starting skill or first job.
- Make the middle steps show how experience builds.
- Make the last step show the likely next level, not a promised destination.
- Mention portfolio, credits, referrals, licenses, auditions, unions, or location only when they matter for that job.
- Keep the pathway realistic for a student reading the site for the first time.

## Program Notes

- Describe the exact program fit.
- Name the degree, certificate, department, or training route when known.
- Do not use program language that makes a school sound like it is better than another school.
- Mention accreditation, licensing, union preparation, language of instruction, or degree level only when it changes how a student should read the listing.
- Do not include conferences, associations, short workshops, or career-development resources in program lists unless they are true training programs.
- When editing a job card, review and rewrite every listed program description for that card, not only newly added or removed programs.
- Do not add editorial add-on sentences telling students what they should do outside the program unless the user specifically asks for that kind of caveat.
- Do not use phrases that rank or compare program fit, such as "strongest for," "best for," "ideal for," or similar claims.
- Do not use "prepares students" or "trains students." Use direct alternatives such as "covers," "teaches," "includes," or "provides training."
- Do not add explanatory fit sentences that start with phrases like "The connection," "The program connects," "These courses connect," or "The connection to." Program descriptions should state the actual curriculum, degree, certificate, or training route and then stop.

## Human Editing Rules

- Replace broad claims with specific nouns.
- Replace hype with facts.
- Cut repeated phrases.
- Remove empty adjectives.
- Prefer "can" and "may" when outcomes depend on portfolio, location, credits, auditions, or hiring networks.
- Do not use "best" unless the user specifically asks for ranked recommendations.
- Do not write like the site is making official advice.
- Do not write like a school brochure.
- Do not write like AI generated the page.
- Do not tell the reader what to think about a job, school, or pathway. Describe what is true and leave out editorial judgment.
- Remove editorial caveats such as "requires focused practice," "requires additional focused experience," "students should," or "would need" unless the user specifically approves that wording.
- If a program is adjacent rather than exact, describe the actual curriculum and stop there.

## AI Expression Patterns To Avoid

Do not use common AI-style setup lines, fake-reveal structures, or moralizing contrast patterns. These patterns can make copy sound manufactured even when the facts are accurate.

- Do not start with broad era framing like "in a world where..."
- Do not write "most people..." followed by "the few who..." or "the best..." as a moral contrast.
- Do not use "stop doing X, start doing Y" phrasing.
- Do not use "not this, not that, but this" sentence structures.
- Do not use FOMO warnings like "if you are not doing X, you are already behind."
- Do not use "the real work is..." or "the real game is..." reveal structures.
- Do not use "you do not need more X, you need Y" minimalist advice phrasing.
- Do not use "it has never been easier/harder" contrast framing.
- Do not use "here is the truth" or "what nobody tells you" reveal framing.
- Do not set up vague dramatic pressure before making a simple point.
- Do not create strawman contrasts just to make a sentence sound deeper.
- Do not turn practical information into motivational advice.
- Do not make the writing sound like a social media productivity post.

## Preferred Replacements

- Instead of hype, use the exact skill, tool, setting, or credential.
- Instead of "career landscape," use "field," "industry," or "job market."
- Instead of "unlock opportunities," use "can lead to work in..."
- Instead of "student potential," use "student goals," "student preparation," or "student work."
- Instead of "showcase," use "show," "list," "include," or "present."
- Instead of "enhance," use "improve," "add," "build," or "support."
- Instead of "leverage," use "use."
- Instead of "streamline," use "make simpler" or "reduce extra steps."
- Instead of "prepares students," use "covers," "includes," "teaches," or "provides training."
- Instead of "trains students," use "teaches" or "provides training."
- Instead of "strongest for" or "ideal for," name the actual program content and avoid the comparison.
- Instead of "requires focused practice," state only the verified program content or leave the caveat out.

## Final Pass Checklist

Before a rewrite is accepted:

1. No em dashes.
2. No banned words unless part of an official name or required source wording.
3. No questions.
4. The first sentence says what the person does.
5. Responsibilities are concrete.
6. Career steps move in a realistic order.
7. No school ranking language.
8. No promises of employment.
9. No repeated stock phrases across nearby cards.
10. No AI-style fake-reveal, FOMO, motivational, or moralizing contrast patterns.
11. No editorial comments, judgments, opinions, or value claims.
12. The writing sounds like a person who has done the research and is explaining it clearly.
13. No "prepares students," "trains students," "strongest for," "ideal for," "matter most," "requires focused practice," "students should," or similar advisory wording.
14. Program descriptions for the edited card have all been checked, not only new additions.
